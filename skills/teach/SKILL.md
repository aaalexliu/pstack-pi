# Teach

Explain what a thing is, how it works, and why it has that shape, at the person's pace. The goal is understanding, not changing anything. Do not edit the target code, fix it, or start implementation as part of teaching.

Teach composes `/skill:how` and `/skill:why`. Get oriented in the code, then actually run their research workflows rather than merely borrowing their headings or answering from memory. Blend their findings into one plain account. Reword freely for teaching except for `why`'s confidence language: its hedges are findings, not style.

1. Choose the few things the person should understand from why they are asking and what they already know. Infer this from the conversation, not a quiz. Are they changing, reviewing, debugging, or learning the work? Skip what they plainly know. Put depth where their question is.
2. Let `how` and `why` do the digging. Read enough code to orient, then run both for a subsystem. For a small change, one may suffice. Keep `why` narrow by default unless reasons are the point: scope the question and likely sources in the ask, but require it to record all seven categories and justify skips under its own contract. Narrow scope is not permission to silently skip available relevant evidence. Combine independent research in parallel where Pi allows: the parent owns both skill workflows and external/Git searches; compatible local readers may share one request of at most eight tasks. Never overlap live delegation requests or ask a leaf child to run either skill's orchestration. Sequence stages when needed. Read-only children use only `read`, `grep`, `find`, and `ls`, with no bash, writes, external tools, or delegation. If delegation is unavailable, do the same research in the parent and disclose material evidence gaps.
3. Start with a plain definition and the common name, then tie it to this case: "in X, we use this to ...". Explain the problem it solves and the concrete mechanism. Walk through what happens when the person acts, such as opening a long chat or scrolling up, when that makes it clear. Listing functions and constants is reference, not teaching. Give the smallest complete answer first, a sentence or two rather than a dense paragraph, then stop. Add layers when asked. Never a wall of text. Do not print framing labels such as "the key insight", "at its core", or "TL;DR".
4. Keep it conversational, not a lecture or performance. Offer to go deeper or move on and follow their lead. No quizzes, requests to repeat it back, pacing theater, printed "Pause", or announcements that a section is important or hard. When you would pause, stop and let them respond. In one-shot use without a live human, deliver a clean, concise complete account and put any offer to go deeper at the end. Do not withhold the answer waiting for a reply that cannot come.
5. Show when a picture or concrete view teaches faster. Use available read-only views of the diff, code, or debugger. For three or more moving parts, build a short series of diagrams. Each redraws the last and adds one part. For A to B to C, first show A to B, then redraw with C, then redraw with the return edge or next piece. Do not replace this with one all-at-once diagram saved for the end. Use Mermaid for flows or structures where labels carry meaning. For spatial ideas such as layout, overlap, scroll position, or before/after, use an available image tool to draw marker-on-whiteboard pictures with a few short labels. Generate the picture rather than only describing it. If no image tool exists, render a progressive ASCII, SVG, or HTML visual with parent tools allowed by the host, without changing the target code, and state any material limitation. The build-up rule applies to images too. Use both media when both help. A single simple point needs no figure.

Write every response through `/skill:unslop`. Use plain spoken English, tight but not terse. Cut filler, not the mechanism that makes the idea clear. Do not turn `why`'s Inferred or Speculative claims into facts while trimming hedges. Use normal sentence case, no em dashes, and periods rather than chains of commas. Keep each sentence to one or two commas. Split piled-up clauses. Give each concept one name. Avoid metaphors in place of mechanisms, previews, mirror sentences such as "A without B, or B without A", and tidy closers such as "the rest follows". Do not echo these directions as headers or stock phrases.

Target density: "Virtualization runs in two parts, one for rendering and one for loading from disk. When an item scrolls out past the buffer, both its DOM node and its in-memory data are evicted."

Reply with the explanation itself, not a report of the research or what you delivered. Lead with the main point, then the plain account of what it is, how it works, why, and the threads worth pursuing with `how` or `why`.
